Apple Could Lose Commission on Third‑Party App Sales

The Legacy of Apple’s App Store Revenue Model  

Since the launch of the App Store in 2008, Apple has collected a standard 30 % cut on most digital goods sold through iOS apps. The model was justified as a means to fund the platform’s curation, security, and distribution infrastructure. Over the years, Apple introduced a reduced 15 % rate for small developers and subscription‑based services after the first year, but the baseline commission remained a cornerstone of the company’s services revenue.

Key points of the original model:

  • Security & Trust: Apple’s review process is marketed as a guarantee against malware and privacy violations.
  • Developer Reach: Access to a global user base of over a billion iPhone and iPad users.
  • Revenue Stream: The commission contributed billions of dollars annually to Apple’s Services segment.

The model, however, has always been a point of friction for developers who argue that the 30 % fee is excessive, especially when competing platforms (e.g., Google Play) have experimented with lower rates or alternative pricing structures.

Epic Games vs. Apple: A Legal Flashpoint  

The 2020 lawsuit filed by Epic Games after its “Fortnite” app was removed from the App Store ignited the most public scrutiny of Apple’s commission policy to date. Epic’s central claim was that Apple’s mandatory 30 % cut constituted an illegal monopoly under antitrust law. The case has been presided over by a single judge whose rulings have shaped the broader conversation about platform economics.

Why the Judge Matters  

  • Precedent Setting: The judge’s decisions could influence how other jurisdictions treat app‑store commissions.
  • Remedies: Potential remedies include forcing Apple to allow “side‑loading” of apps or to open its payment system to third‑party processors.

Epic’s victory in the initial trial forced Apple to allow developers to direct users to external payment options, but the judge later reversed that specific remedy, keeping Apple’s in‑app purchase system largely intact. The back‑and‑forth has left the industry in a state of legal limbo, prompting Apple to reassess its stance on commission structures.

Apple’s New, More Modest Commission Proposal  

In the wake of the courtroom drama, Apple submitted a proposal to the court that suggests a “lower‑commission” framework for sales made through alternative app stores. While the exact percentages were not disclosed in the public filing, the language indicates a willingness to move away from the rigid 30 % model.

Core elements of the proposal  

  • Tiered Rates: A sliding scale that could reduce the commission for high‑volume sellers.
  • Conditional Waivers: Potentially waiving fees for certain categories of apps, such as educational or nonprofit tools.
  • Transparency Requirements: Mandating clearer reporting of fees to developers.

The proposal appears designed to placate the judge while preserving as much of Apple’s revenue stream as possible. However, the same filing also contains a startling admission: Apple may ultimately receive no commission at all from third‑party app store sales.

Regulatory Filings Reveal a Zero‑Commission Possibility  

Apple’s most recent regulatory disclosures, filed with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, acknowledge that the company could end up with zero revenue from sales conducted outside its own ecosystem. This admission is significant for several reasons:

  1. Financial Impact: Services revenue accounted for roughly 20 % of Apple’s total revenue in the most recent fiscal year. A loss of commission income could shave billions off that figure.
  2. Strategic Shift: Accepting zero commission suggests Apple is prepared to prioritize ecosystem openness over short‑term earnings.
  3. Regulatory Pressure: The language aligns with ongoing investigations by the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) and the European Commission, both of which have signaled intent to curb “gatekeeper” behavior.

The filing does not commit Apple to a zero‑commission outcome; rather, it acknowledges the risk if courts or regulators force the company to open its payment pipeline fully.

Industry Impact and Future Outlook  

For Developers  

  • Revenue Forecasting: Developers must now model scenarios where a portion of sales bypasses Apple’s payment system, potentially increasing net margins.
  • Compliance Complexity: Managing multiple payment providers across jurisdictions adds operational overhead.

For Competing Platforms  

  • Opportunity: Third‑party app stores (e.g., Amazon Appstore, Samsung Galaxy Store) could capture a larger slice of iOS traffic if Apple’s commission evaporates.
  • Risk: If Apple ultimately retains a modest fee, the competitive advantage of alternative stores may be muted.

For Consumers  

  • Price Variability: Users might see lower prices for apps that can avoid Apple’s markup, but they could also encounter fragmented purchase experiences.
  • Security Concerns: Apple’s review process is a key defense against malicious software. A shift toward side‑loading could re‑introduce security risks, a point highlighted in recent security‑focused coverage such as the Mac Antivirus Intego One article.

Parallel Revenue‑Sharing Debates  

Apple’s commission controversy mirrors broader industry discussions about revenue sharing. For instance, the recent rollout of X’s Original Content Rewards program, which replaces traditional ad‑based monetization with a creator‑first revenue model, underscores how platforms are rethinking how value is split with creators. The analysis in X’s Original Content Rewards: Creators’ 2026 Guide provides useful context for understanding the shifting economics that Apple now faces.

Frequently Asked Questions

Answer: Apple’s regulatory filing admits the possibility of zero commission, but no definitive policy has been announced. The final outcome will depend on court rulings and potential regulatory mandates.

Q2: How does this affect the Epic Games lawsuit?
Answer: The admission strengthens Epic’s argument that Apple’s commission model is not immutable. However, the judge’s earlier reversal on side‑loading means the case remains complex.

Q3: Are alternative app stores likely to become mainstream on iOS?
Answer: If Apple is forced to allow zero‑commission sales, third‑party stores could gain traction, especially for niche categories. Adoption will also hinge on user trust and the seamlessness of the purchasing experience.

Q4: What does this mean for app security?
Answer: Apple’s review process is a primary line of defense against malware. A shift toward side‑loading could re‑introduce security vulnerabilities, a concern echoed in recent coverage of Mac security tools like Intego One.

Q5: How does this relate to other platform revenue‑sharing models?
Answer: The debate mirrors changes on platforms like X, which are experimenting with creator

The Broader Implications for Big Tech  

Apple’s admission about the potential for zero commission is not occurring in a vacuum. It reflects a growing regulatory and judicial pushback against the business models of dominant tech platforms. Similar pressures are being felt by other major players:

  • Google: Facing antitrust lawsuits in the U.S. and EU over its Google Play Store policies, including its own 30% commission structure. The company has already been forced to allow alternative payment systems in some regions, such as South Korea.
  • Amazon: Under scrutiny for its treatment of third-party sellers on its marketplace, including allegations of self-preferencing and excessive fees.
  • Meta: Investigated for its dominance in social media and digital advertising, with regulators exploring whether its acquisition strategies have stifled competition.

Apple’s situation is particularly notable because it has long positioned itself as a champion of user privacy and security—arguments that have historically justified its walled-garden approach. However, as regulators and courts increasingly view these justifications as pretexts for maintaining monopolistic practices, Apple’s ability to sustain its commission model is being tested like never before.

The Role of Global Regulations  

The outcome of Apple’s commission debate will also be shaped by global regulatory trends:

  • European Union (EU): The Digital Markets Act (DMA) requires Apple to allow third-party app stores and alternative payment systems on iOS by March 2024. Apple has already begun implementing changes, such as allowing developers to use external payment processors, but the DMA’s long-term impact on commission structures remains unclear.
  • United States: The FTC and Department of Justice (DOJ) have ramped up antitrust enforcement, with Apple as a key target. The DOJ’s recent lawsuit against Apple alleges that the company’s App Store policies harm competition and innovation.
  • Asia-Pacific: Countries like Japan and South Korea have introduced or are considering legislation to curb app store monopolies. South Korea, for example, passed a law in 2021 requiring Apple and Google to allow alternative payment systems, though enforcement has been contentious.

These regulatory pressures suggest that Apple’s ability to maintain its commission model may be increasingly limited, particularly in jurisdictions with strict antitrust laws.

What’s Next for Apple and the App Economy?  

The next 12–18 months will be critical in determining the future of Apple’s commission structure. Several key developments to watch:

  1. Epic Games Lawsuit Appeal: The ongoing legal battle between Apple and Epic Games is likely to reach a resolution, either through a settlement or a final court ruling. The outcome could set a precedent for how app store commissions are regulated in the U.S.
  2. DMA Compliance in the EU: Apple’s implementation of the DMA’s requirements will be closely scrutinized. If the EU finds Apple’s changes insufficient, the company could face significant fines or further legal action.
  3. U.S. Regulatory Actions: The DOJ’s lawsuit against Apple could result in structural changes to the App Store, including the forced separation of Apple’s payment processing from its app distribution business.
  4. Developer and Consumer Reactions: If Apple is forced to reduce or eliminate commissions, developers may flock to alternative app stores, while consumers could see changes in app pricing, security, and availability.

Potential Outcomes  

  • Apple Wins: If courts and regulators rule in Apple’s favor, the company may retain its commission model with minor adjustments, such as the tiered rates proposed in its recent filing. This would preserve Apple’s revenue stream but could lead to continued developer dissatisfaction and regulatory scrutiny.
  • Apple Loses: If Apple is forced to allow zero-commission sales or fully open its payment system, the company could see a significant decline in services revenue. However, it may also benefit from increased app diversity and developer loyalty, which could offset some of the financial losses.
  • Compromise: A middle-ground scenario could emerge, where Apple retains a reduced commission (e.g., 10–15%) while allowing alternative payment systems. This would balance regulatory compliance with revenue preservation but could still face legal challenges.

Conclusion  

Apple’s admission that it may earn zero commission from third-party app sales marks a pivotal moment in the company’s history. For over a decade, the 30% commission has been a cornerstone of Apple’s services revenue, funding its ecosystem and justifying its control over the App Store. However, as legal and regulatory pressures mount, Apple is being forced to reconsider its business model in ways that could reshape the app economy.

The outcome of this debate will have far-reaching implications for developers, consumers, and competing platforms. If Apple is forced to abandon its commission model, it could lead to a more open and competitive app ecosystem—but also one with potential security risks and operational complexities. Conversely, if Apple succeeds in preserving its commission structure, it may face continued legal battles and developer backlash.

One thing is clear: the era of the 30% commission is under threat, and the future of the App Store will be defined by the balance between regulation, innovation, and Apple’s ability to adapt.

Q6: How might Apple offset the loss of commission revenue? Answer: Apple could explore several strategies to compensate for lost commission revenue, including:

  • Increased subscription fees: Charging higher fees for services like Apple Music, iCloud, or Apple TV+.
  • Advertising expansion: Growing its advertising business, particularly in the App Store and Apple News.
  • New monetization models: Introducing premium features or services for developers, such as enhanced analytics or marketing tools.
  • Hardware pricing adjustments: Potentially increasing the cost of iPhones or other devices to offset lost services revenue.

Q7: What are the risks of a zero-commission model for developers? Answer: While a zero-commission model could increase developers’ net revenue, it also introduces risks:

  • Security vulnerabilities: Apps distributed outside Apple’s review process may be more susceptible to malware or fraud.
  • Fragmented user experience: Users may face inconsistent purchasing experiences across different app stores, leading to confusion or frustration.
  • Discoverability challenges: Smaller developers may struggle to gain visibility in a more fragmented app ecosystem.

Q8: How does this compare to Google’s approach to app store commissions? Answer: Google has taken a more flexible approach to commissions than Apple, particularly in response to regulatory pressures. For example:

  • Alternative billing: Google allows developers to use third-party payment systems in some regions, such as South Korea, though it still charges a reduced commission (e.g., 11% instead of 15–30%).
  • Lower fees for subscriptions: Google offers a 15% commission for subscription-based apps after the first year, similar to Apple’s policy.
  • Sideloading: Google has historically allowed sideloading on Android, though it has introduced restrictions in recent years to improve security.

While Google’s model is more permissive than Apple’s, it is also facing increasing regulatory scrutiny, particularly in the EU and U.S.

Q9: Could Apple’s admission about zero commission be a negotiating tactic? Answer: It’s possible. Apple’s regulatory filings are designed to manage investor expectations and signal compliance with legal requirements. By acknowledging the possibility of zero commission, Apple may be attempting to:

  • Preempt regulatory action: Demonstrating a willingness to adapt could help Apple avoid harsher penalties or mandates.
  • Pressure developers: Encouraging developers to accept a reduced commission rather than risk a zero-commission outcome.
  • Shape public perception: Positioning itself as a company that is responsive to regulatory and developer concerns.

However, the admission also reflects the very real legal and regulatory risks Apple faces, making it more than just a negotiating tactic.

Q10: What should developers do to prepare for potential changes? Answer: Developers can take several steps to prepare for a shifting app economy:

  • Diversify revenue streams: Explore alternative monetization models, such as subscriptions, in-app advertising, or direct sales.
  • Optimize for alternative stores: If third-party app stores gain traction, developers should ensure their apps are compatible with these platforms.
  • Stay informed on regulations: Keep up-to-date with legal and regulatory developments in key markets, such as the EU and U.S.
  • Invest in security: If sideloading becomes more common, developers should prioritize security measures to protect their apps and users.
